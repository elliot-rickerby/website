elliot rickerby is a cultural researcher and psychologist. blending empirical academic grounding with a passion for fashion, elliot aims to use quality research and scientific understanding to solve problems within the fashion industry. he believes understanding human behaviour is crucial to catalysing progression across all industry areas.

elliot has carried out research for Uniqlo as well as a range of independent designers, alongside contributing to academic papers. his master’s thesis - scheduled for publication in 2026 - marks an early step in addressing the mental health pressures within the fashion industry. he also writes regularly, with a small collection of published articles and a blog where he crtitically analyses modern culture.

To wear or not to wear?

primary research qualitative analysis Consumer Insights Brand Strategy

This collaborative study between UNIQLO, Toray Industries, and London College of Fashion investigated why AIRism innerwear - popular in Asia - underperforms in European markets. Through semi-structured journaling with six UK participants, I used Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis to reveal four themes: material comfort, breathability, confidence linkages, and branding influence.

European consumers prioritised AIRism's breathability during exercise rather than daily wear, diverging from Asian patterns shaped by humid climates. Participants noted cold-weather limitations, suggesting climate-specific expectations. One explicitly valued branded underwear despite limited visibility, connecting to brand preference as a purchase driver. The study positions cultural values, climate adaptation, and modesty norms as explanatory frameworks for consumption disparities, providing actionable insights for Toray's European strategy while establishing groundwork for cross-cultural fashion psychology research.

For this research, I was the recipient of both the Best Presenter and London College of Fashion's Innovative Research Awards.

FASHION, HERITAGE & INTEGRATION

Literature Review Brand Development Identity

This literature review, which I completed for a freelance designer, explored the potential of garment construction as a therapeutic practice for individuals navigating dual heritage or diasporic experiences. Synthesising research across art therapy, consumer culture theory, and trauma studies, it establishes how textile-based activities function as sites of emotional restoration and identity negotiation.

Three interconnected domains emerge: cultural reconnection as intergenerational healing, creativity's role in psychological integration, and heritage-rooted identity's impact on consumption. The review positions pattern cutting as a material metaphor for reconstructing fragmented identity - parallel to textile practices that externalise memory and reclaim cultural agency.

The review identified pattern cutting as having untapped potential as a psychosocial intervention that could foster wellbeing while challenging overconsumption through cultural continuity and fulfilment.

"We're all suicidal; whats the issue?"

Academic Occupational Psychology Critique Industry Culture

This research exposes a systemic mental health crisis within fashion design through 15 semi-structured interviews with designers. The study reveals a culture where 48-hour workdays are normalised, suicidal ideation is dismissed by leadership, and toxicity becomes a professional badge of honour.

Five interconnected themes emerged: educational culture, industry dynamics, the capitalism-creativity tension, survival mechanisms, and reform pathways. Rather than treating fashion as exceptional, the work shows designers experience exploitation patterns similar to other fields - passion weaponised for profit, burnout romanticised as commitment.

The study advocates for structural transformation: reformed production cycles, enforceable workplace policies, and alternative economic models rooted in degrowth principles. By connecting lived experiences to occupational psychology frameworks, it provides academic rigour and practical groundwork for policy intervention, challenging fashion exceptionalism and demanding industry accountability. This study was completed as my Master's thesis.
